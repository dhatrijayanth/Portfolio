Aveeno Bottle Redesign

My response to the prompt "rethink an object or experience": a sustainability redesign of an Aveeno lotion bottle so less product is left behind, recycling is cleaner, and a screwable partition reaches what the pump cannot.

Final product

A cylindrical prototype with a screwable partition. The pump stays for everyday use; the bottle can also open like a lid-based design so lotion at the bottom is not stranded.

Assembled prototype

Cylindrical form with screwable top and bottom halves; pump remains part of normal use.

Access to the bottom

Opening the vessel to reach lotion that would otherwise stay trapped.

Scoop experiment

Alternate path: a scoop at the end of the pump. Less expensive, but not as efficient as the screwable partition.

TL;DR

The problem

Through a sustainability lens, this lotion bottle leaves product stuck at the bottom. That wastes resources and makes recycling harder.

What I did

I redesigned the bottle with a screwable partition for access without leaving lotion exposed to air, shifted the form to a cylinder to fit the screw, and kept the pump for ordinary use. I also tested a cheaper scoop-on-pump alternative.

The outcome

A physical prototype built with support from TyRex Group. Next would be a fully functional product tested for leakage, air exposure, and related issues.

Context

This project answers the prompt to rethink an object or experience. I chose a familiar Aveeno lotion bottle and looked at it through sustainability: climate-minded design starts with waste you can still see when the pump feels empty. Filmed at home, the work asks what fails when you treat the bottle as leftover product and recycling friction, not only as packaging on a shelf.

Problem

When the pump runs dry, lotion is often still sitting at the bottom. You cannot get it out without cutting or discarding the bottle, so product is wasted and recycling gets messier.

Empty pump, product left behind

Pumping with little or nothing coming out while lotion remains inside.

Teardown

Cutting the bottle open to show how much lotion was still trapped at the bottom.

Goals

  1. Let people reach lotion that the pump cannot pull up.
  2. Protect remaining product from unnecessary air exposure.
  3. Keep everyday pumping available, with a path toward less waste and cleaner recycling.

Design decisions

Screwable partition

The decision

Split the bottle with a screwable partition so the bottom half can open when the pump no longer delivers product.

Why

That gives access to stranded lotion while still closing the vessel again, instead of permanently cutting the package open.

Cylindrical form

The decision

Change the bottle from the stock flattened silhouette to a cylinder that can take the screw.

Why

The partition only works if the geometry can accommodate a clean screw join. The cylinder also supports using the bottle like other lid-based designs.

Pump kept, scoop tested

The decision

Keep the pump for normal use, and separately try a scoop attached to the end of the pump as a cheaper alternative.

Why

Familiar pumping should not disappear. The scoop was a lower-cost experiment, but it proved less efficient than opening the screwable partition.

Credits. Prototype made with support from TyRex Group.

Reflection

What I learned

  • Sustainability problems often hide in leftover product, not only in material choice.
  • A teardown that shows trapped lotion makes the waste visible in a way shelf packaging never does.
  • Cheaper alternatives like a scoop are worth testing, even when the main direction is stronger.

What I would do next

  • Build a fully functional version and test for leakage and air exposure.
  • Pressure-test the screw seal through repeated open and close cycles.
  • Keep pushing sustainability into every design decision, not only the concept statement.
